The AI conversation in most boardrooms and news cycles centers on a narrow slice of a much larger system. Most people are talking about ChatGPT. Some are talking about Anthropic or Gemini. Few are talking about what sits underneath all of it, or what will sit above it. That is a mistake.

A useful framework for understanding the full AI landscape is a five-layer stack, each layer enabling the one above it. To understand where AI is going, and more importantly where value will accrue, you need to understand the whole stack.

## Layer 1: Compute and Hardware Infrastructure

This is the foundation. Everything else runs on it.

Layer 1 is dominated by specialized chips, primarily GPUs and custom ASICs, designed specifically for the computational demands of AI workloads. Training large models requires extraordinary amounts of raw compute. Running them at scale requires more. The performance and efficiency of this layer remain a critical bottleneck and a key area of ongoing innovation and capital deployment.

The strategic importance of Layer 1 cannot be overstated. Nvidia is the dominant player, with its H100 and successor chips providing the backbone of AI training infrastructure globally. Taiwan's TSMC manufactures much of this silicon. The US maintains a meaningful lead in both capacity and technical complexity at this layer. China has invested heavily to close the gap but has not yet matched the technical capability of US-developed chips, a reality that continues to shape the trajectory of the global AI race.

Without continued investment in Layer 1, every layer above it stalls. This is not a future problem. It is a current constraint.

## Layer 2: Cloud and Data Infrastructure

Layer 1 provides the chips. Layer 2 provides the environment those chips operate in, and the data pipelines that feed them.

Cloud platforms like AWS, Azure, and Google Cloud provide the scalable infrastructure for training and deploying AI models at enterprise scale. This layer also includes the data platforms that sit alongside cloud compute: companies like Snowflake that are critical for managing, structuring, and preparing the vast amounts of data that AI systems require.

Layers 1 and 2 together represent the unsexy but essential foundation of the AI economy. They are not glamorous. They are not the subject of consumer excitement or media coverage. But they are where a significant share of AI infrastructure investment is flowing today, precisely because the higher layers of the stack cannot scale without them. Before more models can be trained, before more applications can be deployed, this infrastructure must be built.

## Layer 3: Foundation Models

This is the layer most people recognize.

Foundation models are the large-scale AI systems built on top of the infrastructure layers below: large language models, diffusion models, and the frontier AI systems developed by players like OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic. These are the "brains" of the stack. They are trained on massive datasets, at enormous cost, and represent years of research and billions of dollars of capital expenditure.

From a consumer perspective, foundation models are often described as a "black box." That description is largely accurate. Most users do not interact with these models directly. They do not see the training data, the weight updates, or the inference infrastructure. They see a chat interface. That interface is not Layer 3. It is Layer 5.

The leading foundation model players are acutely aware of this distinction. The model itself is the moat. Protecting it, improving it, and controlling access to it through APIs and developer platforms is the primary strategic priority for the companies operating at this layer. The commercial success of these companies depends not just on model quality but on how effectively the layers above and below them are developed and integrated.

## Layer 4: The AI Operating and Orchestration Layer

Layer 4 is the most underappreciated layer in the stack, and arguably the most consequential for the next five years.

This emerging layer sits between the foundation models and the consumer-facing applications above them. Its function is coordination. Platforms operating at Layer 4 connect different models, data sources, and applications to enable the creation of complex, automated, multi-step AI workflows. Think of it as the operating system for enterprise AI: a governance and directional layer that establishes the rules, connections, and context within which AI agents and applications operate.

For a company, the orchestration layer is what transforms disconnected AI tools into an integrated intelligence system. Instead of deploying a chatbot here and a document summarizer there, an orchestration platform enables AI to coordinate across business functions, passing context and outputs between agents, connecting to internal data sources, and automating end-to-end processes that previously required human coordination at every step.

A clear example of what this could look like at scale is Microsoft. Microsoft occupies a uniquely advantaged position in enterprise software. Its products are embedded in the daily workflows of hundreds of millions of people across email, documents, spreadsheets, presentations, project management, and communication. An orchestration layer built on that installed base, connected to foundational models through its existing OpenAI partnership, would give Microsoft the ability to coordinate AI across the full breadth of enterprise operations for its existing customers. No other company has the same combination of enterprise distribution and AI model access. That positioning, if executed effectively, represents one of the most defensible opportunities in the entire stack.

Layer 4 is emerging in real time. The companies that build durable orchestration platforms will sit at a critical leverage point in the AI value chain.

## Layer 5: Agentic AI and Applications

This is the layer that most people interact with every day, even if they do not call it that.

Layer 5 consists of the consumer and enterprise-facing applications built on top of the lower layers of the stack. These applications provide a user interface and a backend connection to foundational models, typically through APIs, to deliver AI capabilities for specific use cases. ChatGPT, Claude, Harvey (legal AI), and Nabla (clinical documentation) are all Layer 5. So are thousands of smaller, more specialized tools built by startups, individual developers, and internal corporate engineering teams.

This is the "wild west" of the AI stack. Applications at Layer 5 are relatively fast to build. A motivated team with access to a foundation model API and a front-end can ship a product in weeks. As a result, tens if not hundreds of new applications appear and disappear every day. Most will not survive. The ones that will are those that develop genuine proprietary data advantages, deeply embedded workflows, or switching costs that transcend the model access they are built on. Being a well-designed wrapper on GPT-4 is not a sustainable competitive position. Being the system of record for an industry's most critical workflow is.

The trajectory of Layer 5 just got materially more interesting. On May 11, 2026, OpenAI launched the OpenAI Deployment Company, a new majority-owned subsidiary capitalized with more than $4 billion in initial investment from 19 firms including TPG, Brookfield, Bain Capital, Goldman Sachs, and McKinsey & Company. The thesis is direct: most companies now have access to frontier AI tools, but few have the engineering talent to integrate them into core operations at speed.

The Deployment Company's model is borrowed from Palantir's playbook. Forward Deployed Engineers (FDEs) will embed directly inside client organizations, working alongside business leaders and frontline teams to identify where AI creates the most value, redesign the workflows around it, and build production systems that stick. To staff the operation immediately, OpenAI simultaneously acquired Tomoro, a UK-based AI consulting firm with approximately 150 experienced engineers already operating inside companies like Tesco, Virgin Atlantic, and the NBA. The Deployment Company's investment and consulting partners, including Bain & Company and Capgemini, collectively work with many thousands of businesses globally, providing OpenAI with a distribution network that is already embedded in the organizations it wants to serve.

The implicit ambition is to formalize the "wild west." OpenAI is not just building models anymore. It is building the implementation engine that will determine how those models get embedded into the operations of the world's largest enterprises.

## The Global Race: US vs. China

The five-layer framework also provides a useful lens for understanding the global AI competition. The United States and China are the two dominant players, and they have taken markedly different approaches across the stack.

At Layers 1 and 2, both countries have invested heavily to build out the compute and data infrastructure required for frontier AI development. The US holds a meaningful advantage at Layer 1. Nvidia's chips, manufactured with significant support from Taiwan, remain technically superior to anything China has been able to develop domestically. China continues to invest in closing that gap, but the gap persists.

At Layer 3, the US has a modest edge in frontier model capability. OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google represent the current frontier. China's DeepSeek has demonstrated that it can approach comparable performance at dramatically lower initial development cost, which has implications for both capital efficiency and accessibility in markets where compute resources are constrained.

The more important divergence, however, is not at Layer 3. It is between Layer 3 and Layer 5.

The US has concentrated its strategic energy and capital on Layer 3, with the ultimate goal of reaching artificial general intelligence (AGI). The prevailing American thesis is that achieving AGI will be a macro-level economic event: a rising tide that elevates productivity, output, and innovation across industries in ways that are difficult to fully anticipate from the current vantage point. The investment calculus is speculative but the potential prize is enormous.

China has taken a different approach. Chinese AI investment has been concentrated at Layer 5, with particular emphasis on industrial and manufacturing applications. The "dark factory" concept is the clearest expression of this: fully automated manufacturing facilities that do not require lighting because they do not require people. This is AI deployed as an operational tool, optimized for specific use cases, integrated directly into physical production systems. It is not glamorous. It works.

Two structural differences compound the strategic divergence. First, China's areas of focus are largely directed top-down through government policy, while US AI development is primarily corporate-directed toward outcomes that maximize shareholder value. Second, every generative AI model operates with content restrictions. In the US, those restrictions are primarily oriented around safety: preventing outputs that could cause harm to users. In China, there is an additional layer of restriction: any content that diverges from CCP-managed views on government, history, or politically sensitive topics is filtered or blocked. Whether this constraint materially impedes the development of Chinese foundation models at Layer 3 remains an open question. At Layer 5, where applications are often purpose-built for specific industrial tasks, the restriction may be less relevant. For general-purpose models competing at the frontier, it is a legitimate variable.

## Where This Leads

The stack is not static. Each layer is being built and rebuilt simultaneously, and the interactions between layers are becoming more complex as the technology matures.

The near-term competition for value will likely center on Layers 4 and 5. Foundation model performance is converging. Infrastructure is being commoditized by scale. The durable differentiation will belong to whoever can build the orchestration platforms that coordinate AI across enterprise operations and the application-layer systems that become indispensable to specific workflows. OpenAI's Deployment Company is a direct bet on that thesis.

The longer-term question is Layer 3. If AGI arrives, and the leading US labs believe it will, the question of who controls the foundational intelligence layer becomes one of the most consequential strategic questions of the century. That is not hyperbole. It is the operating assumption of the companies and governments currently spending billions of dollars to answer it.

Understanding the stack is not optional for anyone serious about navigating this moment. The players are different at each layer. The investment dynamics are different. The competitive moats are different. The geopolitical stakes are different. Collapsing all of it into "AI" misses almost everything that matters.
